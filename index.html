How this works. Applied inches = precipitation rate × run time. Depth = applied inches × the soil's spread factor (clay ≈5 in of wetting per inch applied, loam ≈7, sand ≈12). Aiper IrriSense 2 note. The unit puts out 26 L/min (6.9 GPM) at 45 psi. Precipitation rate = 96.3 × GPM ÷ square feet covered, so the rate depends on how big a map you point it at — full 4,800 sq ft spread gives 0.14 in/hr, a quarter of that gives 0.40 in/hr. At full coverage it applies water slower than clay absorbs it, so runoff is a non-issue and the whole depth goes down in one uninterrupted pass — no cycle-and-soak needed. Total time, though, is set by gallons and not by zoning: one head at one flow rate means zones run back to back, so carving the lawn into more maps doesn't shorten the session. Shortcut for planning: minutes ≈ square feet × inches ÷ 11. Zoning is for giving shade, full sun, and beds different depths — not for saving time. If the app takes a target depth directly, use that and ignore the slider.

Timing

When to water, and why it matters

Two forces work against you at opposite ends of the day: evaporation burns water off before it soaks in, and overnight leaf wetness feeds fungus. There's one window where both are low.

4:00 – 9:00 AM
Best

Water here

Cool air, low wind, minimal evaporation loss. Blades dry within a couple hours of sunrise, so nothing stays wet long enough for disease. Municipal pressure is also at its highest before the neighborhood wakes up.

9:00 AM – 3:00 PM
Wasteful

You'll lose a third of it

Heat and wind evaporate 20–35% of the output before it reaches soil. Not harmful — the myth about droplets scorching blades isn't real — just expensive. Fine for emergency rescue on a crunchy lawn.

3:00 – 7:00 PM
Acceptable

Workable if you must

Evaporation drops off, but the clock is against you. Finish early enough that blades dry before sundown — otherwise you're in the next band without meaning to be.

7:00 PM – 4:00 AM
Worst

This is how lawns get fungus

Almost zero evaporation loss, which is why it's tempting. But grass sits wet for 10–14 hours straight, and that's the exact condition brown patch, dollar spot, and pythium need. Summer humidity in southeast Michigan makes this a reliable way to lose turf.

Cutting height

Mow high, especially in July

Blade height is the cheapest drought tool there is. Taller grass shades its own soil, drops root depth in proportion to leaf height, and cuts evaporation more than any schedule change will.

Deck at 7 means cut at 5.5–6". The one-third rule sets the trigger, not the calendar — in August drought that can stretch to 10–14 days between mows because the grass is barely growing. Footprinting is the gate. If you walk across the lawn and the blades stay pressed down instead of springing back, the plant is water-stressed and mowing adds injury on top of it. Water, wait, check again a few hours later. Only mow when it springs back. Raising the deck is always safe — going from 6 to 7 removes less blade, not more, so the one-third rule doesn't apply when you're going up. Also wait if the lawn is wet. Wet clippings clump, tear rather than cut, and mat down into the canopy.

The one-third rule. Never remove more than a third of the blade in one pass. Cutting a 6" lawn down to 3" strips the food factory and forces the plant to spend root reserves regrowing leaf — it's the fastest way to undo a month of deep watering. If it got away from you, cut it in two passes a few days apart. Sharp blade. A dull blade tears rather than cuts, and the frayed tip loses water and invites disease; the whole lawn looks faintly white a day after mowing. Sharpen every 20–25 hours of run time. Leave the clippings. They're roughly 4% nitrogen and return about one free fertilizer application per season. They do not cause thatch.

Ground truth

The screwdriver test

A long screwdriver or a piece of ⅜" rod pushed into the soil is a better moisture sensor than anything you can buy for under $200. Push it in the same spot each morning, before your watering window.

No readings yet. Push the probe in, then tap the depth it reached — the log fills in from there and each entry tells you whether that was a water day or a skip.

Reading it. Stops at 2–3" → the profile is dry, water today. Reaches 4" → borderline, water if the forecast is hot. Reaches 6" or slides in easily → skip, check again tomorrow. Probe in the morning, not at night — soil dries through the afternoon heat so an evening reading looks drier than what the grass actually has overnight, and you want the call made right before the sprinklers would run. Use one fixed spot in open lawn, away from a sprinkler head and away from edges, so you're comparing the same conditions each time. If it reads easy five days running, the issue is drainage, not schedule.

Calibration

Catch-can test

Every number above is an estimate until you measure your own output. This takes twenty minutes once and makes the rest of it real.

StepDo this
1Set 5–6 straight-sided cans — tuna cans are the standard, any flat-bottomed container works — across one zone. Put some near heads and some at the far edge of the throw.
2Run that zone exactly 15 minutes.
3Measure the depth in each can with a ruler. Average them.
4Multiply by 4. That's your precipitation rate in inches per hour, for real, in your yard.
5Check the spread. If the driest can holds less than half what the wettest holds, you have a coverage problem — a clogged nozzle, a tilted head, or wrong spacing. No schedule fixes that. Brown patches in an otherwise green lawn are almost always this, not underwatering.

Why it beats the chart. Published precipitation rates assume design pressure and correct spacing. Real yards have long hose runs, partly clogged nozzles, mixed head types on one zone, and pressure that sags when the neighbor's system kicks on. A 15-minute test with six cans catches all of it at once.

Metro Detroit calendar

The year

April – May

Growth flush. Mow often, water little — spring rain usually covers it. Pre-emergent for crabgrass goes down when soil hits 55°F, roughly when forsythia drops its flowers.

June – August

Heat and drought stress. Raise the mower to 3.5–4". Water deep, 2×/week, 1–1.5" total. No nitrogen — it pushes growth the roots can't feed. Brown ≠ dead: cool-season grass goes dormant to protect the crown.

Aug 15 – Sep 15

The overseeding window, and the best one of the year. Soil is still warm enough for fast germination, air is cooling, weed pressure has dropped. Core aerate first on clay, then seed into the holes.

September – October

Recovery. Best fertilizer application of the year goes down now — it builds roots rather than top growth. Keep watering new seed lightly and often until it's established, then switch back to deep and infrequent.

November

Final cut at 2.5–3" to reduce snow mold. Blow out irrigation lines before the first hard freeze. Leave shredded leaves on the lawn — they break down over winter and feed the soil.

The rule that outranks all of it

Don't flip-flop. Grass pushed out of dormancy and then abandoned back into it usually doesn't survive the second round. Once you commit to watering through a dry summer, hold it until fall rain takes over.
